# The Jodies of Texas A&M University

John Arthur Kane<br/>
3 July 1981

!!! note "This document has been revised"
    This document was written for an English class at Texas A&M University
    in College Station, Texas. The representation below has been edited to
    reflect changes in time and feeling.

There once was a man whose name was Jody. Jody, probably an African-American,
was very special. It seems that he "got around" a lot. Whenever one of his
neighbors was away, be it in a jail or a war or whatever, Jody seemed to win
the affections of this poor unfortunate's wife, sister, or girlfriend. Not only
would he sleep with the above mentioned but he would also use the car, support
money in effect he would suck a man who was already in the dumps dry. Needless
to say that the residents, male residents anyway, found Jody to be downright
annoying, even if their female kindred had a liking for him.

When our poor citizens were away in jail or at a war they met others who had
Jodies in their neighborhood too. This common enemy and interest led to songs
or chants about this immoral immortal. It is these songs that eventually led to
a whole genre of work songs called "jodies."

A jody is a marching chant or song used in the military to help maintain step
and cadence. When an outfit runs or marches jodies can usually be heard. The
jodies have become specialized and are used at certain tiroes. There are jodies
for marching to chow, jodies for running, jodies for parades, and jodies for
protestation. The jody serves as a vehicle for the soldier, the "folk," to
relieve some of his tensions and anxieties as well as maintaining a cadence for
marching; it would be foolish and untrue to say that everyone who leads or
participates in a jody is trying to relieve tension for many times the jodies
are chanted because they just are f un.†

This paper will deal with one small sector of the folk, the ROTC Cadets at
Texas A&M University. This ROTC branch, the Corps of Cadets, is one of the
largest officer training schools in the nation. Students, while attempting to
gain a college education, go through the routines of a military life while they
either strive for a commision as an officer in the Armed Forces of the United
States or attempt to share in the comraaraderie and fellowship that abounds
throughout the organization. This Corps of Cadets is a seven-day corps which
means that the cadets wear a uniform everywhere they go on campus everyday of
the year. Military training classes are manditory for each cadet. In many
aspects the Corps is the military.

This military persuasion plus the fact that many of the cadets have served in
the military before attending A&M gives Texas A&M access to many military
traditions. Jody managed to migrate over into the lore of the cadets and has
proved his adaptablity to different situations. The jodies used by the cadets
at A&M are probably the most varied and most representative of the entire genre
as can be collected in any one place. All branches of the Armed Forces are
represented in the Corps therefore jodies frcta every branch are in use,
including a few that are specific to A&M.

In order to understand jodies and why they exist as they do one must have an
understanding of the environment in which they are employed. Texas A&M was
founded in 1876 in a barren part of Texas called the Brazos Valley. The most
accesible means of traveling there was by train. This rough land attracted many
rough students. The Agricultural and Mechanical College of Texas was to provide
a practical education for those young men who wanted to become better farmers.
This student body was indeed rowdy. The first students had to cut their hair
short and cut their beards off, buy a uniform, and subject theirselves to
military training and discipline. So was bom the Corps of Cadets.

In the early years of the college a dynamic man by the name of Lawrence
Sullivan "Sully" Ross became president of A&M. This man did much to shape the
character, style, and reputation of the college. Today he is a legend to the
Aggies, as those who attend Texas A&M are prone to be called. Like many other
folk heros Sully is remembered in the lore of the folk. There exist numerous
jodies about Sully and the examples that he has to give to good Aggies abound
in them. In the years since Sully was president there have been many other
people who have given much to the school but none are remembered or revered as
much as Sully is.

Throughout the years of A&M's life there were always students who were not a
part of the Corps of Cadets. Many of those students were either graduate
students or could not participate in the Corpus activities due to medical
reasons. This "non-reg" population, all those who were not in the Corps, had
always been a small percentage of the student body. Today, with an enrollment
of more than thirty-thousand students, the Corps comprises only two-thousand
members. It is no longer an all-male school either. Yet despite this non-reg
majority the Corps is still looked upon as the "gaurdians of tradition" at A&M.
The cadets have an intesive program of "campusology," literally knowledge about
the campus, that is taught to and memorized by every cadet. These formulaic
questions and answers about the history of A&M gives the cadets a better
understanding of A&M and her traditions and serves to instill a strong sense of
pride in each and every one. This pride for the school explains the popularity
of some of the jodies at A&M.

The jodies serve a dual purpose; they maintain cadence and they are used to
show pride, relieve fear, and as a release for many other emotions. How
jodies evolved or first came about is uncertain. Jodies about Jody have
existed 2 since at least 1939 . It is likely that jodies have existed in some
form or fashion ever since man has had a need to march in formation. In that
sense jodies serve the same purpose that work songs for chopping tress or
picking cotton do in the prisons. It is much easier to march when each man
takes steps at the same time. The jody coordinates this very well. There
is a pattern in the tune of the jody that tells each person when to place what
foot down. Long marches or runs can tend to be boring and tedious; jodies
break the tedium and lead to a higher spirit in the men. This allows the men
to march or run longer.

The jodies have also gained a game-playing role. The leader can insult his
surperiors, military life, or anything that comes to mind. At A&M this game-
playing role has taken on added features to acccmidate the college student.
Here the leader can be punished for his jodies by being assigned to do push-ups
or other physical exercises or, at special times, by being ordered to jump into
a mud hole and roll around in it. The jodies are very formulaic and the rhyming
schemes so straight-forward that new jodies can be made up in the pattern and
tune of older ones rather easily. This allows for ready composition of jodies
to fit any occasion. This adaptability and flexibility is in part what allows
jodies to survive.

There are two basic forms of the tempo in the jody: quick-time and double time.
Quick-time is a marching cadence that is about eighty steps a minute, in other
words it is a standard marching tempo. Double-time is the step used for
running. Though double-time is called running it is really a shuffle that
allows 3 the group to run for a greater length of time without becoming overly
tired. Many of the jodies can be sung at both quick-time and double-time
but there are also jodies that can only be sung at one cadence. When a jody is
sung at quick-time the accents in the tune usually occur on every foot with the
stronger accents on the left foot. At double-time the accents usually occur
every other foot, usually on the left foot. This stagering of the accent allows
the jodies to progress at about the same speed at either quick-time or
double-time.

The rhyming scheme of jodies are familiar rhyming patterns found in all
traditional music. The ABAB and the ABCA are the most prevelant. Jodies usually
break logically into stanzas of either two lines or four lines. The leader will
chant the first line and then the group will repeat that line. The leader then
chants the second line and the group repeats it and so on until the jody is
finished. This method is most common though there are jodies that vary from
this. The most frequently occuring variance is one where the leader will chant
a line and the group will have its own response that is distinct from the
leader's lines. The last type of rhyming/performance scheme is one where the
leader will chant a one, two, or three syllable word or phrase that is echoed
by the group. This type has no set order and is used mainly as a filler while
the leader thinks up a new jody.

The following pages contain some of the jodies collected at Texas A&M during
the period of 1 January 1981 to 30 April 1981. This is not a complete
collection but it is one that is representative of the types of jodies in
current use in the Corps. Appendix A contains the texts of the jodies and a
short interpretaion of each jody can be found in appendix B. Appendix C
contains a glossary of some of the terms used in this paper.

## Interpretation

### A&M
This jody is sung at both quick-time and at double-time. This
particular text reflects the pride that the members of the Corps of Cadets
at Texas A&M have in their organization. It also reflects the fact that
throughout most of Texas A & H's history the college was composed almost
entirely of ROTC cadets. Though this has changed in recent history the
jodies continue to hold onto this theme.

### AMP to AMC
This jody is sung at quick-time. This text points out that while the
cadets enjoy being cadets, they also enjoy the summer when they can
become like "real" people. The occurance of "31 days" was an arbitrary
choice. When the jody is sung, the actual number of days until Final
Review is substituted. "AMF to AMC" is an abreviation in cannon use with
the cadets, it means : Adios Mother-Fucker to A&M College. It is used
whenever there is a chance for the cadets to leave the college for "open
weekends," "Corps trips," or school breaks.

The second stanza refers to a train. In the early years of the
college the only way to reach it was by train. The old train depot still
stands off of the west end of the campus.

### Cadence Counts
This jody is sung at quick-time. There are numerous varieties of
cadence counts. They are used to set the proper marching tempo and to
insure that everyone is "in step." This example is of the kind where the
leader has one set of lines while the rest of the group has another set.
This is a classical example of climax of relatives. Stanzas seven, eight,
and nine are commonly incorperated into other jodies as a filler to enable
the leader to come up with another jody. The last four line stanza is used
to remind cadets of the proper arm swing and distance in marching. In this
stanza everyone repeats each line. This is not usually sung with the
previous stanzas but was included here for completeness.

### Chesty Puller
This jody is sung at quick-time. Lewis B. "Chesty" Puller was a
well-known man in military history. He served with the Marine Corps during
the war in nicaraqua and served brilliantly in the Pacific during World War
II. He was involved in Guadalcanal and many of the other campaigns. He
served as Commandant for the recruit training base in Pearl Harbor and was
later elevated to the rank of Brigadier General during the Korean War.
Chesty Puller is used in this jody as a tie-in with history to lend
the jody more substance to the Marine Corps recruit. This jody is typical
of those sung on long marches; it uses a lot of repetition and has a very
formulaic and simplistic verse. It, like the example in Cadence Counts,
also uses climax of relatives.

This jody reflects the image that a young private in the Marine Corps
would have of his superiors, all the way from the generals with visions of
god-hood to theirselves and their party attitude. Open derision and
insults are coinnon and tolerated, i£ not expected, in jodies.

### Columbo
This jody is sung at quick-time. Cadets love to make historical
figures more "real" by giving them traits that are not normally expounded
upon in history texts. Columbo reflects what "really" happened when
Christopher Columbus went to search for a passage to India. This jody uses
the mechanism of a refrain to give it the necessary length to make it
popular for long marches. Length is one of the more important features of
jodies. A leader knowing a few long jodies can go longer than one who knows
several short jodies.

### Howdy
This jody is sung at both quick-time and at double-time, though
more often at double-time. This jody is an example of an outfit jody,
expressing pride in one's outfit. Saying "howdy" is a tradition at Texas
A&M, so it is only natural for a jody to incorperate it in its text.

### In the Corps
This jody is sung at quick-time. This jody uses many of the
mechanisms found in ballads. There is a refrain that has no relation to the
rest of the text, and there is incremental repetition. What sets this jody
apart from others is that it is sung in unison, not like others where the
leader sings a line and the rest of the group repeats the line. Before the
beginning of each verse the leader will yell a clue to the group, such as
"and its cold duck," to cue the rest of the group as to what the rest of
the verse is to be. This jody could last quite some time if the leader can
come up with enough rhyming couplets.

### Jody
This jody is sung at both quick-time and at double-time. The Jody
cadence is one of the most popular jodies in use; in fact the term for this
genre of folk tradition, jody, come frcm this chant. Jody, a contraction
of "Joe the" and "Grinder," is well known in folk tradition. He is the
guy who always gets to stay at home and "gets" your girlfriend and many
other things. Two versions that are in common use at Texas A&M are
presented, in Version A Jody is a big strong guy that the leader cannot
handle whereas in Version B the leader will take his squad with him to
handle this pretemal trouble-maker.

### Little Bird
This jody is sung at quick-time. This jody reflects the "trickster"
and the cruelity locked-up inside of people. Its directness in its attacks
upon a helpless creature helps to relieve the anxiety associated with the
thought of killing or being killed in the military. Any number of verses may
be sung or made up, depending on the leaders imagination or motivation.

### Marine
This jody is sung both at quick-time and at double-time. This jody
immortalizes the image of the Marines. The Marine Corps' history has been
one of selectivity and elitism. The first stanza of the jody refers to
World War I where a few Marine Corps companies served as ground troops
against the Germans. The Germans call the marines "Devil Dogs" because of
their ferocity in combat.

The second stanza is a reference to the Marine recruit's training.
Recruits go to Paris Island, a hell hole in South Carolina, where their
days are long and arduous. The third stanza reflects the fact that the
recruit is expected to become a weapon expert, a human fighting machine.

The fourth stanza has our idealized Marine meeting Saint Peter at
the gates of heaven. The Marine expects, and is willing, to spend the
rest of eternity in hell because of his life as a Marine. He then notices a
"hundred-thousand" other Marines in heaven, an indication that Marines
are welcome in heaven. The is the most moving stanza in the jody.

The last stanza is a stanza dedicated to the faithfulness of the
Marines. It also is a boast about the abilities of Marines. This jody is one
of the most moving and beautiful jodies in current use at Texas A&M.

### Marine Corps Colors
This jody is sung at both quick-time and at double-time. This jody
is an enumeration of the colors in the Marine Corps emblem. This use of
incremental repetition is an aid to Marine recruits in the memorization of
those colors.

### Biggest Moose
This jody is sung at quick-time. It presents a story of a cadet
being chased by a very ugly female Aggie, a Waggie. Kruger Hall, one of
the female dormitories at Texas A&M, is only one of the dormitories used
in this jody.

### Navy Chow
This jody is sung at quick-time. This text reflects the poor food
and poor pay in the Armed Forces. One can substitute "Army" or "Air
Force" for "Navy" in the stanzas. A common substitution at Texas A&M
is "Duncan", the dining hall that serves the cadets. Older versions of
this jody may have "fifty dollars" in place of "hundred dollars" in the last
verse. This reflects the fairly recent raise of military sustenance pay to
one-hundred dollars a month.

### 1944
This jody is sung at quick-time and at double-time. It memorializes
the fact of the active participation of Aggies in the World Wars,
especially in World War n. It also brings to light the inter-school rivalry
with the University of Texas, tu. Those who go to the University of Texas
are called T-sips.

### Airborne Ranger
This jody is sung at quick-time. It ridicules the Airborne Ranger
branch of the Army. This jody is most often sung by Navy-Marine outfits
at Texas A&M.

### Recon
This jody is sung at both quick-time and double-time. This jody is
used as a filler by the leader in many Navy-Marine outfits at Texas A&M.
The reference is to the reconnaissance companies of the Marines.

### Recon Daddy
This jody is sung at both quick-time and at double-time. It refers
to Marine reconnaissance companies. It expresses some of the fears of
paratroopers. A C-130 is a military transport plane, also called a
"big-assed bird," while "jump up, buckle up, shuffle to the door" refer
to the pre-jump steps that are taken by a paratrooper. A constant fear of
a jumper is that his chutes will not open. The position of a "leaning rest"
is the position for push-ups, a common form of discipline.

### Ring-a-ding-doo
This jody is sung at both quick-time and at double-time.

### Soldier
This jody is sung at both quick-time and at double-time. This jody
can have as many verses as there are branches in the Armed Forces. A verse
particular to Texas A&M is the last verse. "D & C" refers to a cadet
who is "Drill and Ceremonies," one who is not pursueing a military contract
but who participates in the activities of the Corps of Cadets.

### Sul Ross
This jody is sung at both quick-time and at double-time though it is
most commonly sung at double time. This jody refers to Lawrence Sullivan
Ross, Brigadeer General, CSA, Governor of Texas, President of the A&M
College. It demonstrates the honor and respect that Aggies hold for him.
It is most commonly sung while an outfit is on a run, in which case the
outfit will run around the statue of Lawrence Sullivan Ross while singing
this jody.

### Tiny Bubbles
This jody is sung at both quick-time and at double-time. This jody
gives a short history of a recruits life. He looses his hair, learns to
"love" his weapon, then he looses his girl to Jody. The Jody cadence is
incorperated into this jody. Any number of verses can be added to this
jody.

### Yellow Ribbon
this jody is sung at quick-time. This is a jody that really has no
meaning. It is fun and is very popular at Texas A&M.
Aggie An Aggie is one who attends Texas A&M and participates in
the traditions and cerironies of the school, it is also used to
refer to the alumni.

## Glossary ##

Aggie War Hymn
:   The school song for Texas A&M.

Air Force Blues
:   The name given to the uniform worn by the Air Force.

AMF to AMC
:   Adios Mother - Fucker to A & H College. This phrase is used
    to signify the times that cadets are free to leave the campus
    for an extended period of time.

Army Khakis
:   The name given to the uniform worn by the Army.

Blues
:   See Air Force Blues.

C-130
:   A standard military transport plane.

Cadence
:   Cadence is the speed that steps are taken while marching or running in formation.

Campusology
:   Knowledge of/about the campus. Campusology is learned by all of the cadets
    while at school. The questions range from trivial landmarks to the
    administrative organization of the school.

Corps
:   See Corps of Cadets.

Corps of Cadets
:   The member of the ROTC unit at Texas A&M University. Also
    known as the Cadet Corps and the Corps.

Corps Trip
:   A Corps Trip is a trip that is sponsered by the Corps of Cadets. These
    trips are usually to a couple of football games out of town. The Corps goes
    and has a parade in the town and then attends the game. These trips are
    looked forward to by the cadets as a time for fun and a break in the
    routine of life.

D&C
:   Drill and Cerimonies. This term is applied to those cadets who are not
    seeking a military commision but who wish to remain in the Corps.

Devil Dog
:   Name given to the United States Marine Corps units during World War n by
    the Germans.

Final Review
:   Final Review is the weekend before final exams in the spring. At Final
    Review the Corps passes in review and then the senior cadets leave while
    the juniors, sophomores, and freshmen cadets put on the uniform of the
    class above them and holds a review for the seniors. At this time the
    cadets have officially gone up a class. Final Review is a time for
    happiness and tears for many who will not see each other again. It is one
    of the most important events for the Corps during the year.

Howdy
:   A salutation that is distinctive to A&M. A&M prides herself on the
    friendliness of her student body, everyone should say Howdy to each other
    on the campus and promote that friendliness.

Jodies
:   Jodies are chants or songs that are used in order to maintain cadence while
    marching or running. The name is derived from Jody.

Jody
:   Jody is a contraction of "Joe" and "the Grinder." Jody is a familiar figure
    in Negro folk liturature as the person who gets to stay at home all the time
    and takes advantage of another man's wife/girlfriend/sister.

Khakis
:   See Army Khakis.

Kruger Hall
:   A female dormitory on the capus at A&M.

N-1
:   A company sized Navy/Marine option unit in the Corps.

Neanderthals
:   The nickname for company N-1. See also N-1.

Parris Island
:   The training camp for marines located ten miles south of Charleston, North Carolina.

ROTC
:   Reserve Officer Training Course. The ROTC program was designed to train
    college students as officers of the Armed Forces of the United States and
    to function as a conmisioning program for the services.

Sully
:   Lawrence Sullivan Ross, a former president of Texas A&M.

t-sip
:   Name given to those who attend the University of Texas, the arch rivals of Aggies.

tu
:   The University of Texas.

Waggie
:   Name given to females at A&M. More commonly applied to females in the Corps.
